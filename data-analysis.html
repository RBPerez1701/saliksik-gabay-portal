One reading program, three possible questions

  • Quantitative. How much did the same learners’ reading scores change from baseline to endline?
  • Qualitative. How do learners describe what helps them attempt an unfamiliar passage?
  • Mixed methods. How do the accounts of learners with different reading gains help explain the pattern in their scores?

How to narrow the choice

Begin with one research question. Write down what you want to learn, who or what will supply the evidence, and when you can collect it. Consider these routes.

  • How many learners reached the benchmark? Use descriptive statistics. Count learners who met it and give the total number assessed.
  • Did reading scores improve? Find out whether the scores belong to the same learners. For matched scores, open the paired-samples t-test guide and check the distribution of the individual changes. For independent groups, start with the independent-group guide.
  • What made participation easier? For interview accounts, use the thematic analysis guide to interpret shared meanings. For a practical inventory of supports that families requested, start with qualitative content analysis.
  • Why were gains uneven? If scores will guide whom you interview, start with explanatory sequential mixed methods. If interviews will help you develop a survey, start with exploratory sequential mixed methods.
